Purpose & Performance — S&P 500 Capstone

RAG Scoring Claude Python Regression SEC EDGAR

The Problem

Do purpose-driven companies actually perform better financially, or is "purpose" mostly marketing language? The goal was to quantify organizational purpose across all 500 S&P 500 companies from public data and test whether it correlates with financial results.

Approach

Purpose was scored with a RAG system that reads each company's disclosures and website content and rates three dimensions 0–5, combined with an evidence-quality factor and an overlap penalty to stop recycled citations from inflating the score. I built the data collection and the performance-side analysis:

  • Data collection — pulled company and workforce data from LinkedIn and financial data from SEC EDGAR and PitchBook for all 500 companies
  • Purpose index — Purpose Articulation, History Consistency, and Strategy Alignment combined into a single 0–100 score
  • Performance metric — EBITDA margin plus ROIC minus WACC, regressed to strip out company size and industry, then time-weighted across multiple years
  • Presentation — built and delivered the sample-selection and data-sources section of the final deck

Results

  • A statistically significant but weak positive correlation between purpose and performance across the full sample
  • The relationship is concentrated in Financials (n=70, coefficient 0.65, p<0.001) and statistically absent in most other sectors
  • Predictive tests — this year's purpose score against next year's and 5-year-out performance — showed the same weak, scattered signal, not strong enough to stand alone as a predictor

Reflections

The result was genuinely mixed: purpose helps, but mostly in one sector, and the NLP rubric under-detects purpose in technical or legal filing language — Qualcomm scored 2/100 despite strong operations. The useful skill here was communicating that honestly instead of overselling a clean finding.
