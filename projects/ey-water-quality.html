EY Data Challenge — Clean Water Supply Optimization

Remote sensing · Climate data · Feature engineering · RandomForest · Team Project

The Problem

Using satellite imagery and climate data, predict three water quality indicators — Total Alkalinity, Electrical Conductance, and Dissolved Reactive Phosphorus — at around 140 sampling locations across South Africa. Ground-truth water quality measurements are expensive and slow to collect at scale; if satellite spectral bands and climate variables can reliably predict these indicators, water quality could be monitored far more widely and cheaply than manual sampling allows.

My Contribution — Feature Engineering

I worked on the spectral and climate feature engineering layer of the pipeline, building on Landsat satellite bands (NIR, green, SWIR16, SWIR22, NDMI, MNDWI) and TerraClimate variables (PET — potential evapotranspiration):

  • Interaction features: NDMI × MNDWI, PET × NDMI, PET × MNDWI
  • Ratio features: SWIR22/NDMI, NDMI/MNDWI, SWIR22/PET
  • Additional spectral and climate features: green/SWIR22 ratio, SWIR16/SWIR22 ratio, and PET interactions with green, SWIR16, and SWIR22 bands

I then ran an ablation study — training a separate RandomForest model with each new feature added on top of the baseline feature set, and comparing R² across all three targets to check whether each engineered feature actually helped. The honest takeaway: most additional features moved R² by less than a point in either direction, which matters because it's easy to add features that look clever but don't earn their place in the model.

Modeling & Results

The final pipeline used a RandomForestRegressor for each target, with a standard train/test split and feature scaling. Training performance was strong across all three targets, but test performance dropped noticeably — a sign of overfitting given the fairly small sample size (~140 locations):

  • Total Alkalinity: R² train 0.90, R² test 0.55
  • Electrical Conductance: R² train 0.92, R² test 0.58
  • Dissolved Reactive Phosphorus: R² train 0.88, R² test 0.53

Reflections

What I Learned

  • How to construct interaction and ratio features from remote sensing spectral bands and climate variables, and why raw bands alone often correlate weakly with a downstream target
  • How to run a proper ablation study — testing each new feature in isolation against a fixed baseline, rather than adding a pile of features and hoping the model sorts it out
  • How to read the gap between train and test R² as a diagnostic for overfitting, not just a report card on model quality

Limitations

  • A small sample size (~140 locations) limits how much the model can generalize, which shows up directly in the train/test R² gap
  • Most of the additional engineered features gave marginal, inconsistent gains — suggesting the ceiling on this feature set had largely been reached with the baseline spectral and climate variables

Next Steps

  • Try feature importance analysis to identify which spectral/climate variables actually drive RandomForest splits, rather than testing new features one at a time by trial and ablation
  • Test regularized models (e.g. ridge/lasso) or gradient boosting to see whether the train/test gap narrows with a different model family

Tech Stack

  • Python: pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn (RandomForestRegressor, StandardScaler, train_test_split)
  • Remote sensing data access: rioxarray, rasterio, xarray, pystac-client, planetary-computer, odc-stac
  • Visualization: matplotlib, seaborn

Why This Project Matters

This project is where I first worked hands-on with remote sensing data rather than tabular business data — pulling spectral indices out of satellite imagery and pairing them with climate variables to predict something that's normally measured by hand in the field. The ablation study habit I built here — test one feature at a time against a fixed baseline — is something I've carried into every project since.
